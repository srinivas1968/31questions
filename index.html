2. What is the objective of User Acceptance Testing (UAT)? How is UAT different from QA/System Testing? Explain a UAT test case.

From my experience, QA and UAT serve different purposes even though both are essential phases of software quality.

QA or System Testing is performed by the QA team their focus is making sure the application works as designed — validating functionality, identifying defects, and confirming the system behaves correctly according to the documented requirements.

UAT, on the other hand, is performed by business users with support from the UAT team after QA is complete. The focus is on validating that the solution supports the actual business process and meets business expectations.

For example, in one of my projects, the business needed all customer‑facing forms updated from “BMO Harris Bank N.A.” to “BMO Bank N.A.” A UAT test case for that requirement wasn’t just checking one screen. I validated the Account Verification page, generated PDFs, forms in the Forms Library, customer emails, and any other communication where the legal entity name appeared. I also ensured the underlying account verification workflow continued to function correctly after the branding change.

So for me, UAT is about validating the end‑to‑end business flow, confirming the experience from a user’s perspective, and making sure the solution is ready for real‑world use

3. Explain the UAT Test Management Process from planning through test closure.

The UAT Lifecycle is a structured process used to ensure that a solution meets business requirements and is ready for production deployment.

It begins with Requirement Analysis, where I review business requirements, identify business processes, clarify gaps, and establish traceability through the RTM.

Next is UAT Planning, where I prepare the UAT Test Plan, define the scope, schedule, resources, risks, dependencies, and entry and exit criteria.

During UAT Preparation, I ensure that test scenarios, test cases, test data, the UAT environment, and business users are all ready before execution begins.

The next phase is UAT Execution, where business users execute test cases while I coordinate testing, monitor progress, and support issue resolution.

Any defects identified are managed through the Defect Management process, including defect logging, triage, retesting, and regression testing.

Throughout execution, I perform Monitoring and Reporting by publishing daily status reports, tracking progress, defects, risks, and issues, and communicating updates to stakeholders.

Once testing is complete, I move to UAT Closure, where I validate the exit criteria, prepare the UAT Exit Report, document any residual risks, and obtain business sign-off.

Finally, we assess Production Readiness. Based on the UAT results, I recommend whether the solution is ready for production, while the final go-live decision is made by the business after reviewing the UAT outcomes and accepting any remaining business risks.

4. What are the typical UAT Entry Criteria?UAT Entry Criteria are the predefined conditions that must be met before User Acceptance Testing can officially begin.

From my experience, UAT only runs smoothly when certain conditions are in place, so before I begin UAT, I always verify a few key things.

First, the business requirements need to be fully approved, and the earlier testing phases should be completed with no critical defects outstanding. I also make sure the UAT environment is stable, accessible, and configured correctly, and that the required test data is prepared and validated.

On the preparation side, I confirm that test scenarios, test cases, and the RTM are reviewed and approved, business users are identified and have the right access, and everyone understands the defect management process. Finally, the UAT test plan and schedule should be agreed upon by all stakeholders.

Once these pieces are in place, I’m comfortable recommending that UAT execution can officially begin.

5. What is included in a UAT Exit Report, and how do you determine UAT readiness for production deployment?

From my experience, the UAT Exit Report is essentially the final summary of everything that happened during UAT and a recommendation on whether the solution is ready for production. It usually includes the UAT objectives, the scope we covered, a clear test execution summary, pass/fail metrics, requirement coverage, and a defect summary with any outstanding issues or deferred items. I also include risks, validation of exit criteria, and the final business sign‑off.

Before I recommend production readiness, I make sure all critical business scenarios have been executed, exit criteria are fully met, and there are no open high‑severity defects. If any lower‑severity defects remain, they must be documented and formally accepted by the business. I also confirm that business users have validated the solution and that any required regression testing is complete.

Once all of that is in place and the business is comfortable with the residual risks, I’m confident recommending that the application is ready for production deployment.

6. What is the difference between a Test Strategy and a Test Plan?

From my experience, a Test Strategy and a Test Plan serve two different purposes.

A Test Strategy is the high‑level direction for how quality will be ensured on a project. It sets the overall approach — things like the testing objectives, the types of testing we’ll perform, the environments we’ll use, the tools, the defect management process, and the governance model. It’s more of a guiding framework that stays consistent throughout the project.

A Test Plan is much more execution‑focused. It translates that strategy into a practical roadmap for the team. It outlines what will be tested, who will test it, the timelines, the scope in and out, the entry and exit criteria, the dependencies, the risks, the test data needs, and the reporting structure.

In simple terms, the Test Strategy tells us how we approach quality, while the Test Plan tells us how we execute testing day‑to‑day.

7. Explain what is RTM.

From my experience, an RTM — Requirements Traceability Matrix — is one of the most important UAT artifacts because it helps ensure complete coverage. I use it to map each business requirement to the corresponding functional requirement, test scenarios, test cases, and any defects raised during execution.

It gives me a clear view of what’s been tested, what’s pending, and whether every requirement has been validated end‑to‑end. The main purpose is simple: make sure nothing is missed. When the RTM is complete and fully updated, I’m confident that UAT has covered all the business requirements.

8. What types of gaps or issues do you look for while reviewing requirements?

When I review requirements, I’m not just checking what’s written — I’m looking for anything that could create confusion later in UAT or lead to defects in production. From experience, the most common gaps are incomplete or ambiguous requirements, missing business rules, unclear calculations, undefined exception flows, and missing validations.

Requirement: “System should calculate property tax automatically.”

Gap / Ambiguity:

  • What formula?
  • Which tax rate?
  • Does it vary by province?
  • Should it round up or down?
  • What happens if tax data is missing?

 

Requirement: “System should send a notification when the mortgage application is approved.”

Gap / Ambiguity:

  • What type of notification? Email? SMS? In‑app?
  • Who receives it — applicant, co‑applicant, broker?
  • What is the exact approval stage? Conditional or final?
  • What template should be used?

 

How you would explain it: “I would raise this because the calculation logic is not defined. In banking projects, unclear financial formulas lead to major defects during UAT.”

How you would explain it: “I would flag this because the requirement doesn’t specify the notification type, recipients, or the exact approval stage. Without clarity, UAT cannot validate the communication triggers.”












I also pay close attention to integration points, security and access rules, reporting needs, notifications, and compliance or audit requirements, because these are often overlooked. Another important check for me is testability — if a requirement can’t be objectively validated, I work with the Business Analyst to refine it before we move forward.

Catching these gaps early makes a huge difference. It prevents rework, avoids delays during UAT, and reduces the risk of business‑impacting issues after deployment.

9. How do you begin requirement analysis when you join a new project?

When I join a new project, I start requirement analysis by first understanding the business domain and the key workflows. I review all available documentation such as the BRD, functional specs, user stories, acceptance criteria, process flows, solution design, and interface details.

I then schedule knowledge‑transfer sessions with the Business Analyst and Product Owner to clarify objectives, assumptions, dependencies, and any areas that need deeper understanding.

During this review, I identify business rules, integrations, user roles, validations, calculations, exception handling, reports, notifications, and regulatory requirements. Based on this, I create an initial Requirements Traceability Matrix and outline high‑level business scenarios that will later evolve into detailed UAT test cases.

My goal is to build a clear, end‑to‑end understanding of the business process before moving into test design.

10. How do you test when requirements are incomplete, unclear, or ambiguous?

When requirements are incomplete or unclear, I never proceed based on assumptions. I first identify and document the exact areas of ambiguity. Then I discuss them with the Business Analyst, Product Owner, or business SMEs to get clarification.

If testing needs to continue before clarification is finalized, I document the temporary assumptions, share them with stakeholders, and get their agreement so everything remains traceable.

Once the requirement is clarified, I update the test cases, RTM, and any impacted documentation to reflect the final expected behaviour. This approach avoids rework and ensures full transparency.

Example:

In one mortgage project, the rejection communication workflow was not clearly documented. Instead of guessing, I flagged the gap, discussed it with the BA and business team, and documented the interim assumption. Once the requirement was updated, I revised the test cases and validated the correct workflow. This prevented incorrect testing and ensured alignment with business expectations.

Catching it upfront prevented a production issue and reinforced why detailed requirement analysis is so important before testing begins.

12. Can you walk me through the defect management process you follow in HP ALM?

From my experience, the defect management process in HP ALM starts the moment I find an issue during UAT. Before logging anything, I always make sure the issue is reproducible and not caused by incorrect test data or an environment problem.

Once I confirm it’s a valid defect, I log it in ALM with a clear summary, detailed steps to reproduce, expected vs actual results, severity, priority, environment details, and screenshots. Good documentation upfront saves a lot of back‑and‑forth later.

After logging, the defect is assigned to the development team. They analyze it, apply a fix, and update the status. When the fix is deployed, I retest the scenario and run any required regression to make sure nothing else was impacted.

If the issue is resolved, I close the defect. If not, I reopen it with updated evidence.

Throughout the cycle, I track defect aging, severity trends, and blockers during daily triage meetings. This helps keep everyone aligned and ensures defects are being addressed in the right order based on business impact.

13. Can you explain the defect life cycle you follow in your projects?

From my experience, the defect life cycle is simply the path a defect follows from the moment I log it until it’s fully resolved. In most of my projects, especially using HP ALM, the flow is fairly standard.

When I find an issue, I log it as New with clear steps, expected vs actual results, and evidence. Once the defect is reviewed, it moves to Assigned, meaning a developer or team has taken ownership. When they start working on it, the status becomes Open or In Progress.

After the fix is completed, the developer marks it as Fixed, and it comes back to me for Retest. If the fix works, I mark it Verified and then Closed. If it doesn’t work, I Reopen it with updated evidence.

Sometimes defects are Rejected if they’re not valid, or Deferred if they’re low‑priority and planned for a future release.

Throughout the cycle, I track defect aging, severity, and blockers during daily triage meetings to make sure issues are being resolved in the right order based on business impact.

14. Can you describe your experience managing defect triage during UAT?

In most of my UAT projects, defect triage was a daily activity. My role was to make sure every new defect was reviewed, validated, prioritized correctly, and assigned to the right development team.

During triage meetings, we focused on the business impact of each defect — whether it was reproducible, what severity it should carry, and what the realistic target fix date should be. I also monitored defect aging, followed up on overdue items, coordinated retesting once fixes were deployed, and ensured regression testing was completed when needed.

If a Critical or High‑severity defect impacted a key business process, I escalated it immediately to the Project Manager and business stakeholders so they could make informed decisions about timelines and production readiness.

My overall goal in triage was always to maintain transparency, keep business and technology aligned, and ensure defects were resolved efficiently and in the right order based on business priority.

Strong communication and proactive engagement with business users is essential — it’s what ensures UAT reflects real business needs and supports a smooth go‑live.

18. Can you give me a brief overview of how you handle Business Sign‑Off during UAT?

Business sign‑off is the final confirmation from stakeholders that the solution meets their operational needs and is ready for production. As a UAT Lead, I make sure stakeholders have complete visibility before signing off. I present the UAT Exit Report, requirement coverage, defect summary, outstanding risks, and a clear Go/No-Go recommendation.

If sign‑off is at risk — for example, when I identified the missing rejection notification workflow in a mortgage project — I escalate early, get alignment, and ensure the gap is resolved before deployment.

Once stakeholders approve, I document sign‑off through the exit report and formal communication, and transition the project into deployment readiness and early production support.

19. Can you provide an example of a Risk, Mitigation, and Contingency Plan?

A practical example comes from the BMO Branch Pinpads project. One of the major risks was that the physical pinpads required for UAT might not arrive on time. The delivery was postponed three times, and when they finally arrived, they were the wrong model and had to be returned.

Risk

Delays in receiving the correct pinpad devices could push the UAT start date and reduce the available testing window.

Mitigation Plan

I worked closely with the technology team to track delivery status, escalate delays early, and ensure everyone had clear visibility of the risk. Meanwhile, I prepared all UAT prerequisites — test cases, data, and environment readiness — so that once the correct devices arrived, we could begin testing immediately without losing time.

Contingency Plan

If delays continued, the plan was to prioritize critical scenarios first, extend testing hours if needed, and request additional support to recover time. Fortunately, once the correct devices were delivered, we executed all planned test cases and still met the UAT timelines without impacting the release schedule.

This example shows how early risk identification, proactive communication, and structured mitigation/contingency planning help keep UAT on track even when external dependencies cause delays.

  • Prioritize critical device‑dependent scenarios first once the correct hardware arrived.

  • Extend testing hours or add weekend execution to recover lost time.

  • Request additional testers to accelerate execution if needed.

  • If adequate testing could not be completed, recommend adjusting the release timeline.

Fortunately, once the correct pinpads arrived, we completed all device‑dependent test cases and still met the UAT timelines without impacting the release schedule.

21. Explain the Home Financing (Phoenix) project at BMO.

BMO launched the Home Financing (Phoenix) project to modernize its mortgage approval process and strengthen integration with First National Financial’s Merlin portal. Merlin is used by mortgage brokers to submit applications, track deal status, and communicate with underwriters. Because BMO relied heavily on Merlin for mortgage processing, accurate financial calculations and workflow validation were critical.

My responsibility as a UAT resource was to validate end‑to‑end mortgage workflows processed through Merlin, ensure financial calculations matched BMO’s algorithms, and confirm that communication triggers, emails, and integration points behaved exactly as expected. I worked closely with First National Financial’s mortgage team, who provided detailed test data and calculation rules.

I logged into Merlin using read‑only access and validated each processed mortgage application against the expected outcomes provided by business users and documented requirements. This included checking:

  • Workflow progression through all expected mortgage stages

  • Mortgage‑related emails generated on behalf of BMO

  • Financial values such as down payment, taxes, investment property rules, and multi‑dwelling unit calculations

  • Communication triggers at approval, conditional approval, and decline stages

  • Data exchange between Merlin and BMO’s integrated systems

I also performed financial reconciliation, integration validation, regression testing, and business process verification to ensure the entire mortgage lifecycle behaved correctly.

My testing helped identify several calculation discrepancies and workflow gaps early, preventing potential production issues. By validating Merlin’s outputs against BMO’s business rules, we ensured accurate mortgage processing, correct communication to brokers and customers, and a smooth integration between First National Financial and BMO systems. This contributed to a successful UAT cycle and supported a stable go‑live for the Phoenix project.

23. How do you close UAT when there are known open defects? How do you obtain business sign-off and document residual risks?

Closing UAT with known open defects is possible as long as the business formally accepts the risk. Before closure, I prepare a clear summary of all outstanding defects, including severity, business impact, workarounds, and planned resolution dates.

I review these items with business stakeholders, Product Owners, and the Project Manager to confirm whether the remaining issues are acceptable and do not affect critical functionality, financial calculations, regulatory requirements, or customer experience. If the business agrees, the defects are classified as residual risks.

I document this in the UAT Exit Report along with a Residual Risk Register that lists each open defect, its impact, workaround, risk level, and the business owner who is accepting the risk.

Once everything is documented and reviewed, I obtain formal business sign‑off. The business owns the decision to accept risk, and my role as UAT Lead is to provide full transparency so they can make an informed go‑live decision.

24. What will be your approach if the requirement changes in between execution?

Requirement changes during UAT do happen, and my approach is to handle them in a controlled and structured way.

First, I clarify the nature of the change — whether it’s a true requirement change, a defect, a misunderstanding, or a scope change. If it’s a legitimate change, I work with the Business Analyst and Product Owner to document the updated requirement and understand its impact on development, testing effort, timelines, and release scope.

Next, I perform an impact analysis to identify which business processes and test cases are affected, whether new test cases or regression testing are needed, and whether the RTM and UAT schedule need updates.

I then present the impact to the Project Manager and business stakeholders so they can decide whether the change should be included in the current release or deferred through change control.

Once approved, I update the UAT artifacts, communicate the change to the testing team, execute the revised test cases, and ensure the UAT Exit Report reflects the final agreed‑upon scope.

My priority is always to maintain traceability, avoid uncontrolled scope creep, and ensure stakeholders fully understand the impact before implementation.

25. What would you do if the system works exactly as documented, but during UAT you find that it doesn’t support the actual business process?

This situation comes up quite often, and it’s one of the main reasons UAT is so important. If I find that the system works exactly as documented but still doesn’t support the real business process, the first thing I do is sit with the business users to understand the gap. I ask them to walk me through the actual workflow and explain where the solution is falling short.

Once I understand the issue, I compare it against the documented requirements and acceptance criteria. If the system is behaving exactly as written, then it’s not a defect — it’s a missed or incomplete requirement.

At that point, I bring the Business Analyst, Product Owner, and key stakeholders together to confirm whether this is a missing business rule or a change in the business process. If everyone agrees the solution doesn’t meet the operational need, I recommend raising a change request so the requirement and scope can be updated. We also assess the impact on timelines, testing, and release planning.

If the gap affects a critical workflow or introduces business or regulatory risk, I escalate it to the project leadership so they can make an informed decision before go‑live.

Throughout the process, I make sure everything is documented — the gap, the discussions, the decisions, and the approvals — so there is full traceability.

UAT isn’t just about checking whether the system meets the specification; it’s about confirming that the solution actually works for the business. If it doesn’t, even when technically correct, it needs to be addressed.

26. You have raised a showstopper issue. How will you complete the rest of the test cases and still align with the project timelines?

If I raise a showstopper during UAT, my first step is to assess the impact instead of stopping all testing. I identify which business processes and test cases are blocked, and which ones are independent and can still be executed. I immediately continue with all non‑blocked test cases so the team maintains momentum while the defect is being worked on.

I log the defect with the highest severity, notify the Project Manager, Product Owner, and development team, and push for an urgent triage discussion to confirm timelines for the fix.

Once the fix is deployed, I prioritize retesting the showstopper, executing all previously blocked test cases, and performing regression testing on impacted areas to ensure nothing else was affected.

If timelines are at risk, I provide a clear impact assessment — remaining testing, risks, and schedule implications — so leadership can decide whether to extend UAT or adjust the release plan.

My goal is always to keep testing moving wherever possible while ensuring critical functionality is fully validated before go‑live.

27. You are two days away from UAT completion, 40% of testing remains, and a key feature such as Transaction History is not available for testing. How would you handle the situation and ensure timely closure of UAT?

Two days before UAT completion, if 40% of testing remains and a key feature like Transaction History is still unavailable, this is a critical Issue.

Impact: Around 40% of UAT cannot be executed. Business users are unable to validate a core banking function, increasing the risk of releasing untested functionality into production.

Immediate action: I would immediately inform the Project Manager, Product Owner, Development Lead, and business stakeholders. I would obtain an updated deployment timeline, continue executing all unaffected test cases, reprioritize testing activities, and schedule additional testing resources if required. I would also monitor the issue through daily defect and project status meetings.

Contingency Plan: If the feature cannot be delivered in time for adequate testing, I would prepare a formal residual risk assessment describing the untested functionality, business impact, customer impact, regulatory implications, and available workarounds. During the Go/No-Go meeting, I would recommend extending UAT or postponing the production release if the functionality is business critical. If the business decides to proceed, I would ensure that the residual risk is formally documented and accepted by the authorized business owner.

28. What actions do you take when the project status changes to red?

When a project moves to Red status, my first step is to understand the root cause rather than just reporting the color change. I assess whether the issue is due to critical defects, environment instability, delayed development, limited testing time, or business resource constraints.

Once the cause is clear, I work with the relevant stakeholders to create a recovery plan. This may include prioritizing high‑impact scenarios, reallocating resources, accelerating defect resolution, adjusting timelines, or requesting additional support.

I document the risks, impacts, and mitigation actions clearly and communicate them to project leadership. From there, I provide frequent status updates and track progress until the project stabilizes or leadership makes a decision on scope or schedule changes.

My focus is always on transparency, proactive communication, and protecting the quality of the release.

29. What QA documents and testing artifacts have you prepared during the testing lifecycle?

Throughout the testing lifecycle, I prepare and maintain several key QA and UAT artifacts to ensure proper planning, execution, and traceability.

At the start of the project, I review all requirement documents — BRD, functional specs, user stories, acceptance criteria, solution designs, and process flows — to understand the scope. Based on this, I prepare the Test Strategy and Test Plan, outlining the approach, timelines, scope, resources, risks, and entry/exit criteria.

To ensure full requirement coverage, I create and maintain the Requirements Traceability Matrix (RTM), mapping each requirement to test scenarios and test cases. I then develop high‑level test scenarios and detailed test cases, including preconditions, steps, expected results, and required test data.

Before execution, I coordinate test data preparation and confirm environment readiness.

During execution, I maintain all core UAT artifacts such as Daily Status Reports, Test Execution Reports, Defect Logs, RAID Logs, Risk Registers, and Defect Metrics. I also facilitate defect triage meetings and publish dashboards showing progress and release readiness.

At the end of UAT, I prepare the UAT Exit Report, summarizing execution results, coverage, defect statistics, outstanding risks, and the final recommendation for go‑live.

These artifacts ensure transparency, governance, and complete traceability throughout the testing lifecycle.

30. What are the key UAT dependencies on the Technology Team, and how do you manage them?

UAT has several critical dependencies on the Technology Team because they provide the technical foundation required for testing. The key dependencies typically include:

  • UAT environment availability and stability
  • Application deployment
  • Interface availability
  • Database refreshes
  • Test data preparation
  • User access provisioning
  • Batch jobs and scheduled processes
  • Defect fixes
  • Build deployments
  • Third-party system availability
To manage these dependencies, I maintain a dependency tracker, conduct regular readiness reviews, and discuss outstanding items during project status meetings. I proactively follow up with development, infrastructure, database, release management, and middleware teams to ensure deliverables are completed according to schedule.

If any dependency is delayed, I immediately assess the business impact, update the project RAID log, communicate the risk to stakeholders, and work with the Project Manager to develop mitigation and contingency plans.

My objective is to identify dependency risks early rather than allowing them to become project issues.
